    Red flags
    in medical tourism for plastic surgery

    The real warning signs aren't about the country you're weighing. They're about what a clinic tells you before surgery.

    Guides · noon Clinic team · Updated October 10, 2026

    Something put you on edge before you started searching red flags in medical tourism for plastic surgery: a headline, a complication someone posted about, a relative who heard a story secondhand and passed it along with a warning attached. Fair enough. Here's the piece that actually matters once you start digging, though: none of it comes down to the country stamped on your itinerary. It comes down to one specific clinic, and what it tells you, asks of you, and shows you before anyone picks up a scalpel, whether you're looking at Medellín, somewhere else in Colombia, or another country entirely.

    Red flags in medical tourism for plastic surgery start with what you're told

    Comparing countries is the wrong question. The right one is what a clinic requires before it schedules your surgery, and what it skips. A licensed operating room either exists or it doesn't, regardless of the city. A pre-op lab either gets ordered or it doesn't. Those are things you can check. A country's general reputation isn't.

    No written quoteWhatever isn't itemized upfront tends to show up later as an extra charge
    Date before examYou get a surgery date before anyone has actually examined you
    Photos with no contextBefore-and-afters that don't say who operated, where, or what the real outcome was

    This isn't just a gut feeling. The CDC reviewed deaths of US citizens following cosmetic surgery abroad between 2009 and 2022 and found a pattern: in the cases investigators studied closely, the leading cause wasn't anesthesia or the country itself. It was fat embolism and blood clots, almost always in patients who'd had liposuction combined with fat transfer to the buttocks in the same long surgery, with risk factors that hadn't been screened beforehand. That's exactly what serious pre-op labs and a real in-person consultation exist to catch. They're not paperwork. They're the part that actually changes the risk.

    Phrases that should set off an alarm

    A few of these will sound familiar before you finish the sentence. That's not a coincidence of bad luck or an awkward translation. It's a sales script, and a sales script and a sound medical decision rarely come from the same conversation.

    • "Limited spots if you book this week." Surgery isn't a decision you make against a countdown timer.
    • "Guaranteed results, no scarring, no risk." Every procedure carries risk. Whoever tells you otherwise isn't informing you, they're selling you.
    • "No need to come in first, just send photos." No clinic worth trusting clears a procedure without examining you in person.
    • "Thousands of happy patients." Without a name, a registry, or anything checkable behind it, that's just a sentence.
    • "We can operate the same day you land." A real consultation, labs, and time to decide don't fit between the airport and the operating room.

    What you can actually verify, not just take on faith

    In Colombia, there are concrete registries you can check yourself instead of relying on what you're told. RETHUS (the Ministry of Health's national registry of healthcare professionals) confirms whether a doctor's plastic surgery specialty is genuinely registered to them. REPS does the same for facilities: it says whether a location is actually licensed for surgery. The SCCP (the Colombian plastic surgeons' association) is different from both: it's a private professional group, not a government body, so it's a useful reference among peers, but it doesn't certify anyone or replace RETHUS.

    If you're weighing a different country, ask what its equivalent to those three registries is. If no one at the clinic can name it clearly, that's already a red flag, before you've checked anything else.

    How we answer these questions at noon, in Medellín

    At noon, surgery happens in the licensed operating rooms at Q2, never in an office. Before that, Dr. Fernando Ruiz or Dr. Valeria Enciso examines you in person. WhatsApp is for questions and scheduling; the consultation itself is always in person. If your pre-op labs aren't clear, the procedure gets postponed, no exceptions, even if that means moving your flight.

    And there's something we'd rather say outright, even when it's not what a patient wants to hear: sometimes the right answer is that you shouldn't have surgery, or not yet. Once you're back home, we keep checking in by WhatsApp or video call, but that has an honest limit: it doesn't replace the in-person follow-up visits your surgeon schedules before you fly, and a real emergency means your local emergency room, not waiting on a video call.

    What this checklist can't promise you

    Checking these boxes tells you a clinic is legitimate. It doesn't tell you a procedure is right for your specific case, and that only gets decided in an in-person consultation that looks at your history and your labs, not on a web form. Even at the most careful clinic, surgery still carries risk: bleeding, infection, healing problems, anesthesia complications, among others. Doing your homework doesn't make that go away. What changes is who explains those risks before you decide, and who's accountable if something doesn't go the way you expected.

    If Colombia is the country you're actually weighing, it's worth reading whether it's safe to get plastic surgery in Colombia, and once you have a specific surgeon's name, how to verify them before you book. You can walk through the same checklist on our safety page (in Spanish), with us or with any other clinic on your list. And if one of these red flags has already happened to you, message us on WhatsApp before you cancel everything. Sometimes the fix isn't giving up on the trip, it's switching clinics.

    Frequently asked questions

    Frequently asked questions

    What are the most common red flags in medical tourism for plastic surgery?

    A quote with no written breakdown, getting a surgery date before anyone examines you, before-and-after photos with no verifiable context, guaranteed-result language, and pressure to pay fast. None of these are about the country. They're about the clinic.

    Is plastic surgery abroad riskier than getting it done in the US?

    Not simply because you cross a border. Risk goes up when surgery happens outside a licensed operating room, without required pre-op labs, or without an in-person exam by the person who'll actually operate, and all three of those can also happen without leaving the US.

    What should you ask before booking cosmetic surgery overseas?

    Exactly where the procedure happens and whether that facility is licensed for surgery, who examines you in person before anything is scheduled, what pre-op labs they require and what happens if something comes back abnormal, and who's responsible if something doesn't go as expected, abroad or once you're home.

    How do you verify a clinic abroad is legitimate?

    Get the full legal name of the person who would operate and check whether their specialty is registered with the country's official health authority (in Colombia, that's RETHUS), and whether the facility is licensed for surgery (in Colombia, REPS). If no one can give you those names, that's a red flag on its own.

    What if you already booked and still have doubts?

    Ask before you fly, not after. Get the answers to what's bothering you in writing, and if something can't be explained clearly, you still have time to move the date. A clinic worth trusting would rather field an uncomfortable question before your flight than a complaint after.
