    Before you decide

    All about laser hair removal

    What it is and what people call it

    Laser hair removal uses a highly concentrated beam of light that is absorbed by the pigment in the hair. That light turns into heat, damages the follicle and slows hair growth. According to the American Society of Plastic Surgeons (ASPS), the most common areas are the face, underarms, legs, bikini area and the rest of the body. Many people search for it as permanent hair removal, but the ASPS makes clear that it reduces hair and doesn't remove it completely: the hair that grows back is finer and lighter, and there is less of it. And on women's faces, according to the American Academy of Dermatology (AAD), hormones keep it from being permanent. That's why we talk about gradual, long-lasting reduction.

    Darker skin and light hair: what changes

    In the past, according to the AAD, it could only be done safely on light skin with dark hair. Today it is also an option for brown or dark skin, and for light hair on light skin, although in these cases it has to be done very carefully. On darker skin, the ASPS advises using a less intense laser so the outer layer of the skin isn't damaged. Your results also depend on the area, the color and thickness of the hair, and the growth phase it's in. Your doctor chooses the type of laser and its intensity after examining your skin.

    Who it's not for (or not yet)

    In some cases it's better to wait or to consider another option:

    • A recent tan, whether from the sun, a tanning bed or a spray tan: the ASPS advises waiting until it fades, because the pigment in your skin absorbs the laser light and makes it less effective.
    • White or gray hair: it may not have enough pigment for the laser to work, according to the ASPS.

    Tell your doctor if you tend to scar, if you've ever had a keloid, if you get cold sores or genital herpes outbreaks, or if you've taken isotretinoin (a medication for severe acne): the AAD says your doctor needs to know this before you start.

    When another option makes more sense

    Laser hair removal works only on hair. Many people say “laser” for several different treatments, so it helps to clear that up:

    • Sun spots, texture, pores or fine lines: laser skin treatment or chemical peels, depending on your skin.
    • Melasma or dark spots that keep coming back: a melasma and dark spot treatment plan.
    • Clogged pores or oily skin: a medical facial.

    If you want to work on several things, at your consultation we'll tell you what makes sense for you and in what order.

    Risks you should know about

    It isn't surgery, but it is a medical procedure and it has risks. The AAD and the ASPS list these, among others:

    • Discomfort, swelling and redness: these are the most common. They're usually mild and go away in a few days.
    • Blisters, crusting or infection.
    • Cold sore outbreaks.
    • Changes in skin color, lighter or darker, especially on brown skin. They are usually temporary, but some are permanent.
    • Scarring or changes in skin texture.

    The AAD warns that in inexperienced hands it can cause burns, permanent changes in skin color and scars. Before you start, we go over each risk with you and you sign an informed consent form.

    Preparing for treatment and aftercare

    Once you decide to start, according to the AAD and the ASPS:

    • Don't tan, either in the sun or in a tanning bed, and don't use self-tanner.
    • Wear a broad-spectrum, water-resistant sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher every day. Under the Medellín sun, don't skip it.
    • Don't pull hair out by the root, with tweezers or wax: the laser needs to find it in the follicle.
    • Tell your doctor what medications you take, including over-the-counter ones like aspirin.

    After each session, your skin will be red and swollen, like a mild sunburn; a cold compress helps. You can go on with your day, keeping direct sun off the area and staying away from tanning beds.

    FAQ

    Common questions

    Is it permanent?

    It gives you a significant, long-lasting reduction; you may need maintenance sessions.

    Does laser hair removal hurt?

    According to the ASPS, there is mild discomfort that lasts a few seconds, and the AAD says many people compare it to warm pinpricks or a rubber band snapping against the skin. If the area is small and your skin is very sensitive, your doctor may recommend a numbing gel. Don't use numbing creams on your own: the FDA warns that over-the-counter creams with high amounts of lidocaine, used over large areas, on irritated skin or covered with plastic wrap, can cause serious side effects.

    How often are laser hair removal sessions?

    It depends on the person, and your doctor schedules each session based on how your skin and hair respond. Hair grows in cycles and the laser only works on hair that is in its growth phase, which is why you usually need 6 to 8 sessions or more.

    Can I wax or tweeze between sessions?

    It's best not to. Waxing and tweezing pull the hair out by the root, and according to the ASPS, pulling it out before treatment makes the laser less effective because it has nothing left to target. Your doctor will tell you how to manage hair between sessions.

    Can I sunbathe while I'm getting laser hair removal?

    For as long as your treatment lasts, it's best not to tan or use self-tanner, and to wear sunscreen every day, according to the AAD. If you come in with a tan, the ASPS advises waiting until it fades. After each session, keep direct sun off the treated area and stay away from tanning beds.
