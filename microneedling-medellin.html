    All about microneedling

    Microneedling, Dermapen, or dermaroller: what the names mean

    Microneedling, also called collagen induction therapy, is the treatment. Dermapen and dermaroller are the names of some of the devices used for it, and many people search for the treatment by those names. According to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), these devices have many small, thin needles that prick the skin, and they can be a roller, a stamp, or a pen-shaped tool. The goal of these controlled micro-punctures is to trigger your skin's own repair process and collagen production. Some devices also add radiofrequency to the needles, and the FDA notes that these carry different risks. Your aesthetic physician chooses the device and the needle depth based on your skin.

    What it's used for and what it can be combined with

    It's mainly recommended for acne scars, enlarged pores, uneven texture, and lack of firmness. The FDA has authorized some devices, for adults 22 and older, to improve the appearance of facial acne scars, wrinkles on the face and neck, and surgical scars on the abdomen. Active ingredients or platelet-rich plasma (PRP) can be added, a combination the American Academy of Dermatology (AAD) mentions for acne scars. The FDA points out that it hasn't reviewed the safety or effectiveness of these combinations. Whether to combine, and with what, is up to your physician.

    When another option may be a better fit

    Microneedling mainly targets texture and acne scars. If something else is your main concern, one of these may help more:

    • Dark spots or melasma: a plan for dark spots and melasma, which starts by finding out what's causing them.
    • Sun damage or fine lines: laser treatments.
    • Active acne: chemical peels.
    • Dull or dehydrated skin: skin boosters.
    • Mild sagging, if that's the main issue: collagen biostimulators or radiofrequency.

    At your consultation, we'll tell you which one, or which combination, makes sense for you.

    Who it's not for, or when to wait

    According to the FDA and the AAD, it may not be right for you, or it may be better to wait, if:

    • You're pregnant or breastfeeding.
    • You take blood thinners or aspirin, or you have a bleeding disorder.
    • You have a weakened immune system, uncontrolled diabetes, or a history of eczema, psoriasis, vitiligo, or keloids.
    • You have an active cold sore, a skin infection, or painful or pus-filled pimples.
    • You're taking, or recently took, isotretinoin (often known as Accutane), the oral acne medication.
    • You have a tan or were out in the sun recently.
    • You're getting chemotherapy or radiation therapy.

    At your consultation, we review your medical history and tell you whether it's right for you.

    Risks you should know about

    Every procedure has risks, and this one does too. The FDA lists:

    • More common: redness, tightness, dryness, itching, peeling, burning, bruising, bleeding, and scabbing.
    • Less common: lighter or darker patches of skin, lines on the face, a cold sore flare-up, swollen lymph nodes, infection, or burning and itching when you apply makeup, moisturizer, or sunscreen.

    According to the FDA, most of these show up shortly after the session and go away, but some can be permanent. The AAD adds that the risk of infection, scarring, or lasting discoloration is higher when the person doing it isn't trained. Before you decide, your physician walks you through each one.

    How to prepare and aftercare

    Tell your physician what medications you take, especially blood thinners or aspirin, whether you're allergic to topical anesthetics, and whether you tend to get cold sores. According to the AAD, you may need to follow a skin care routine before your session, which sometimes includes stopping retinol. The FDA suggests asking whether a new needle cartridge is used for each patient and each session.

    Afterward, redness usually lasts 1 to 3 days. Your skin may be more sensitive to the sun and to products with retinol, glycolic acid, or alcohol. Avoid tanning and protect your skin from the sun. Your physician will tell you when you can wear makeup again.

    FAQ

    Common questions

    Does it hurt?

    Your skin is numbed first with a topical anesthetic, and the sensation is tolerable.

    How many microneedling sessions will I need, and how often?

    Usually 3 to 4 sessions, each about 45 minutes. Your aesthetic physician decides the exact number and the time between sessions based on your skin and how it responds. Results show up gradually.

    How long do microneedling results last?

    Results build gradually and vary from person to person. The FDA notes that the improvement may be temporary and that maintaining it may require more sessions over time. According to the AAD, protecting your skin from the sun and not smoking help your skin lose less collagen.

    Does microneedling work for all skin types?

    The AAD says it's safe for most people and for all skin tones, though no treatment is right for everyone. The FDA, on the other hand, warns that in darker skin there's a risk of the skin turning darker or lighter. That's why your physician examines your skin at the in-person consultation before recommending it.

    Can I do microneedling at home with a dermaroller?

    The FDA hasn't authorized any medical microneedling device for over-the-counter sale. According to the AAD, at-home products aren't designed to puncture the skin, and using them the wrong way can irritate it, cause infection or scarring, and spread viruses such as those that cause warts or herpes. It should be done by a trained healthcare professional.
