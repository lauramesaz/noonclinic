Red flags in medical tourism for plastic surgery start with what you're told

Comparing countries is the wrong question. The right one is what a clinic requires before it schedules your surgery, and what it skips. A licensed operating room either exists or it doesn't, regardless of the city. A pre-op lab either gets ordered or it doesn't. Those are things you can check. A country's general reputation isn't.

This isn't just a gut feeling. The CDC reviewed deaths of US citizens following cosmetic surgery abroad between 2009 and 2022 and found a pattern: in the cases investigators studied closely, the leading cause wasn't anesthesia or the country itself. It was fat embolism and blood clots, almost always in patients who'd had liposuction combined with fat transfer to the buttocks in the same long surgery, with risk factors that hadn't been screened beforehand. That's exactly what serious pre-op labs and a real in-person consultation exist to catch. They're not paperwork. They're the part that actually changes the risk.

Phrases that should set off an alarm

A few of these will sound familiar before you finish the sentence. That's not a coincidence of bad luck or an awkward translation. It's a sales script, and a sales script and a sound medical decision rarely come from the same conversation.

  • "Limited spots if you book this week." Surgery isn't a decision you make against a countdown timer.
  • "Guaranteed results, no scarring, no risk." Every procedure carries risk. Whoever tells you otherwise isn't informing you, they're selling you.
  • "No need to come in first, just send photos." No clinic worth trusting clears a procedure without examining you in person.
  • "Thousands of happy patients." Without a name, a registry, or anything checkable behind it, that's just a sentence.
  • "We can operate the same day you land." A real consultation, labs, and time to decide don't fit between the airport and the operating room.

What you can actually verify, not just take on faith

In Colombia, there are concrete registries you can check yourself instead of relying on what you're told. RETHUS (the Ministry of Health's national registry of healthcare professionals) confirms whether a doctor's plastic surgery specialty is genuinely registered to them. REPS does the same for facilities: it says whether a location is actually licensed for surgery. The SCCP (the Colombian plastic surgeons' association) is different from both: it's a private professional group, not a government body, so it's a useful reference among peers, but it doesn't certify anyone or replace RETHUS.

If you're weighing a different country, ask what its equivalent to those three registries is. If no one at the clinic can name it clearly, that's already a red flag, before you've checked anything else.

What this checklist can't promise you

Checking these boxes tells you a clinic is legitimate. It doesn't tell you a procedure is right for your specific case, and that only gets decided in an in-person consultation that looks at your history and your labs, not on a web form. Even at the most careful clinic, surgery still carries risk: bleeding, infection, healing problems, anesthesia complications, among others. Doing your homework doesn't make that go away. What changes is who explains those risks before you decide, and who's accountable if something doesn't go the way you expected.
